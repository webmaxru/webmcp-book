# Business Writing

Use this module for memos, strategy documents, decision papers, proposals,
leadership updates, professional email, project briefs, PRDs, and operating
documents.

The goal is not "executive tone." The goal is to reduce the amount of work the
reader must do to understand what matters.

## 1. Establish the reader outcome

Before editing, answer:

- Who is reading?
- What do they already know?
- What must they understand?
- What decision or action, if any, is needed?
- What is the minimum evidence required for that decision?

If there is no action, do not invent one.

## 2. Lead with what matters

For decision-oriented documents, surface the recommendation, decision, request,
or changed fact early.

Avoid creation-order structure:

background -> analysis -> more analysis -> conclusion

Prefer reader-order structure:

answer -> why -> evidence -> tradeoff -> action

This is a default, not a universal law. A strategy narrative may need some
context first if the answer is unintelligible without it.

## 3. Make the ask inspectable

If asking for a decision, make the request explicit enough that the reader could
answer yes/no or choose among real options.

Weak:
> We'd welcome leadership support as we move forward.

Stronger:
> We are asking for approval to run a three-month pilot with two teams.

Do not create owners, dates, or scope that were not in the source.

## 4. Separate fact, interpretation, and recommendation

A strong business paragraph often contains all three, but the reader should be
able to tell them apart.

Example shape:

> Deployment failures rose from 2.1% to 4.8% after the May release. Most of the
> increase comes from one validation step. We recommend reverting that change
> before the next release.

Fact -> interpretation -> recommendation.

Do not let "we believe" conceal a missing fact.

## 5. Use minimum sufficient evidence

Do not include every analysis artifact because it exists.

For each chart, metric, example, or paragraph ask:

> What decision does this evidence help the reader make?

If none, move it to an appendix, remove it, or explain its purpose.

## 6. Name the tradeoff

Recommendations become more credible when they acknowledge the real cost.

State the meaningful tradeoff:
- speed vs. quality;
- scope vs. timing;
- local optimization vs. shared platform value;
- short-term cost vs. long-term savings;
- autonomy vs. control.

Do not create fake symmetry. Some options are simply worse.

## 7. Use concrete operators

Prefer:
- who;
- does what;
- by when;
- with what dependency;
- measured how.

Avoid hiding the operation behind "alignment", "enablement", "transformation",
or "optimization" unless those terms refer to defined work.

## 8. Write for skimming without turning everything into bullets

A reader should be able to skim:
- title;
- opening;
- headings;
- first sentence of paragraphs;
- charts/tables;
- ask/decision.

But do not over-format. Dense meaning in clean prose is often easier to read
than dozens of labeled bullets.

Use bullets for:
- true lists;
- parallel options;
- actions;
- criteria;
- comparisons.

Use prose for:
- argument;
- causal reasoning;
- nuance;
- narrative.

## 9. Cut status language

Remove phrases whose main job is to sound professional:

- "ongoing commitment to excellence";
- "strategic initiative";
- "exciting opportunity";
- "drive meaningful impact";
- "ensure alignment";
- "unlock value";
- "continue our journey".

Keep them only when they name a real internal program or concept.

## 10. Leadership updates

A useful update usually answers:

- What changed?
- Why?
- What is the impact?
- What are we doing?
- What, if anything, is needed from leadership?

Do not spend half the update re-explaining the project.

## 11. Strategy writing

Strategy requires choice.

Look for:
- a diagnosis;
- a choice;
- what will not be done;
- the mechanism connecting the choice to the outcome;
- risks and dependencies.

A collection of goals is not a strategy.

Do not turn a vague strategy into a sharp one by inventing choices. Flag the
missing decision.

## 12. Proposals

A proposal should make clear:
- problem;
- proposed change;
- why this approach;
- scope;
- cost or tradeoff;
- decision required;
- success criteria.

Not every proposal needs all sections as headings. The content matters more
than the template.

## 13. Professional email

Put the reason for the email near the top.

Avoid:
- "I wanted to reach out";
- "I hope this email finds you well" when it is merely filler;
- long context before the ask;
- "happy to discuss" as a substitute for a clear next step.

Preserve relationship-appropriate warmth.

## 14. Titles and headings

A title should tell the reader what the document is about and, when useful, what
kind of decision it supports.

Prefer informative headings over generic ones such as:
- Background
- Overview
- Discussion
- Next Steps

when a more specific statement is available.

## 15. Final business-writing check

Ask:
- Can the reader identify the point quickly?
- Is the ask or decision explicit?
- Is the evidence sufficient but not excessive?
- Are facts separated from interpretations?
- Is the tradeoff visible?
- Are abstractions hiding actual work?
- Is the requested action clear?
- Did editing preserve uncertainty and political nuance where they matter?
