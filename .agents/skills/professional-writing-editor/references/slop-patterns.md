# Slop Patterns

This is a diagnostic catalog, not a banned-word list.

A pattern matters when it is repeated, unearned, or carrying work that the
underlying thinking should carry. One occurrence rarely matters.

## 1. Empty framing

Symptoms:
- "It is important to note..."
- "There are several key factors..."
- "In today's rapidly changing..."
- "When it comes to..."
- announcing what will be discussed before discussing it.

Test:
> Does the sentence add information, or only announce importance/context?

Preferred response:
Delete it, or replace it with the actual claim already present.

## 2. Significance inflation

Symptoms:
- pivotal, transformative, groundbreaking, profound, critical, revolutionary;
- "marks a significant shift";
- "underscores the importance";
- "a testament to".

These terms are not forbidden. The question is whether the evidence earns the
magnitude.

Preferred response:
State the observable change and let the reader infer its importance.

## 3. Manufactured contrast

Symptoms:
- "It is not X. It is Y."
- "Not just X, but Y."
- "The real issue isn't X; it's Y."
- repeated contrast structures where X was never a serious alternative.

Preferred response:
State Y directly unless the contrast carries real argumentative work.

## 4. Generic connective tissue

Symptoms:
- moreover;
- furthermore;
- additionally;
- importantly;
- consequently;
- "that said" used reflexively.

Preferred response:
Delete the connector and inspect what remains. If the relationship becomes
unclear, fix the order or reasoning rather than finding a stronger transition.

## 5. Reflexive rule of three

Symptoms:
- every list has three items;
- a third item duplicates or generalizes the first two;
- rhythmic triads appear repeatedly.

Preferred response:
Keep the number of ideas that actually exist.

An earned triad is fine.

## 6. Vague authority

Symptoms:
- "research shows";
- "experts agree";
- "studies suggest";
- "it is widely recognized";
- "industry leaders believe".

Preferred response:
Name the source when known. Otherwise qualify the statement or flag the missing
evidence.

Do not invent a citation.

## 7. Abstraction stacks

Symptoms:
- "strategic alignment opportunities";
- "transformation capabilities";
- "process optimization framework";
- multiple nouns standing in for actors and actions.

Preferred response:
Ask who did what, to what, and with what consequence.

## 8. Padded verbs / copula avoidance

Symptoms:
- serves as;
- stands as;
- acts as;
- represents;
- provides a way to;
- enables the ability to.

Preferred response:
Use the plain verb when it preserves meaning.

## 9. Generic positive closers

Symptoms:
- "the future looks bright";
- "the possibilities are endless";
- "this positions us for continued success";
- "ultimately, X will be key";
- a final paragraph that only re-summarizes.

Preferred response:
End on the last useful implication, decision, unresolved question, or concrete
next action.

## 10. Performed candor

Symptoms:
- "let's be honest";
- "here's the thing";
- "the truth is";
- "I have seen this a hundred times" when no such experience was supplied;
- casual profanity or dramatic fragments inserted to appear human.

Preferred response:
Do not simulate personality. Preserve real personality when it exists.

## 11. Rhetorical-question scaffolding

Symptoms:
- "So what does this mean?"
- "Why does this matter?"
- "But what if there were another way?"
used as default transitions.

Preferred response:
State the answer directly unless the question is genuinely useful to the
reader's thought process.

## 12. Generic reader flattery

Symptoms:
- "As you know..."
- "For forward-thinking leaders..."
- "Savvy teams understand..."
- "Whether you're a beginner or an expert..."

Preferred response:
Remove status signaling. Speak to the reader's actual context.

## 13. Parallelism without content

Symptoms:
- repeated "we need to..." clauses;
- identical sentence stems across a paragraph;
- lists whose grammatical symmetry exceeds their conceptual symmetry.

Preferred response:
Preserve useful parallelism, but do not let symmetry manufacture equivalence.

## 14. Uniform rhythm

Symptoms:
- every sentence within a narrow length band;
- every paragraph has nearly identical shape;
- repeated short-short-long or long-long-short patterns;
- every paragraph opens with a topic sentence and closes with a mini-summary.

Preferred response:
First fix structure. Then vary rhythm only where the existing cadence feels
mechanical.

Random variation is not quality.

## 15. Formatting scaffolds

Symptoms:
- `**Label:** explanation` repeated for every bullet;
- many tiny sections;
- a heading every few sentences;
- repeated "Key takeaway", "Why it matters", "What this means" boxes.

Preferred response:
Use formatting only when it helps navigation or comparison.

## 16. Excessive hedging

Symptoms:
- perhaps, potentially, arguably, somewhat, generally, often, likely;
- several qualifiers attached to one claim.

Preferred response:
Distinguish epistemic hedges from social padding.

Keep uncertainty that changes the truth of the claim. Remove hedges that merely
avoid commitment.

## 17. Excessive certainty

The opposite failure also matters.

Symptoms:
- "clearly";
- "obviously";
- "undeniably";
- "proves";
- "will" where the source only supports "may".

Preferred response:
Restore the source's actual level of certainty.

## 18. Self-congratulatory adjectives

Symptoms:
- exciting;
- innovative;
- best-in-class;
- powerful;
- elegant;
- robust;
- seamless.

These can be correct. Treat them as claims that need evidence, not decorations.

## 19. Generic examples

Symptoms:
- invented placeholder anecdotes;
- "Imagine a company that...";
- examples that could fit any industry;
- analogies that add tone but no explanatory value.

Preferred response:
Use a real example from the source or flag the need for one.

## 20. AI-vocabulary clusters

Words such as "delve", "tapestry", "landscape", "foster", "underscore",
"pivotal", "showcase", and "leverage" can become recognizable when clustered.

Do not ban them mechanically.

Ask whether each is the most precise word available in this author's register.

## False-positive guardrails

Do **not** flag as AI merely because the prose contains:

- an em dash;
- a semicolon;
- a triad;
- passive voice;
- a transition word;
- a long sentence;
- an abstract noun;
- formal vocabulary;
- parallel structure.

These are normal writing devices.

The diagnostic unit is the pattern across a passage plus whether the writing is
doing real intellectual work.

## The removal test

For any suspicious sentence:

> If I delete this, what exactly does the reader lose?

If the answer is "nothing," it is likely padding.

If the answer is a claim, qualification, transition, or voice-bearing detail,
the sentence is doing work. Edit carefully.

## The anti-humanizer rule

Never replace AI slop with "humanizer slop":

- fake first-person anecdotes;
- forced hot takes;
- random sentence fragments;
- gratuitous rhetorical questions;
- casual slang that does not match the author;
- manufactured emotional stakes;
- conspicuous punctuation variation.

The goal is not irregularity. The goal is authored thought.
