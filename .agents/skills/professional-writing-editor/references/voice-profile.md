# Voice profile

Method: `professional-writing-editor`, especially its `references\voice-calibration.md`

## Compact fingerprint

> An enthusiastic, hands-on peer explains technical ideas through shared exploration, mixing detailed reasoning with short conversational checkpoints, precise terminology with everyday language, and practical recommendations with candid limitations, playful asides, and attention to the reader's experience.

The strongest recurring voice is a practitioner teaching alongside the reader, not an impersonal reference manual or a uniformly terse executive writer. The author is visible: choosing an approach, trying it, reporting what happened, explaining the compromise, and inviting the reader to participate.

This is an editing aid, not a personality profile, a trained model, or a set of phrases to insert mechanically.

## Evidence, scope, and confidence

Technical teaching is the best-supported register. Community advice, opinion writing, personal reflection, formal endorsement, and program announcements have narrower or more context-dependent support.

This is a sample-derived public-writing profile, not a claim that every trait is a permanent preference or applies to every audience. Historical style evidence does not establish current facts.

Confidence labels below mean:

- **High:** recurring evidence across multiple distinct samples, within the stated scope.
- **Moderate:** recognizable evidence, but concentrated in particular contexts or expressed inconsistently.
- **Provisional:** one main sample, uncertain individual attribution, or a register not directly represented.

These are qualitative judgments, not statistical scores. No sentence-length targets, vocabulary percentages, or punctuation quotas have been inferred.

Confidence describes support in the samples, not explicit confirmation of a personal preference. Authorizing use of this guide does not confirm every inference; silence is not confirmation. Treat explicit corrections as stronger evidence, not exceptions to a fixed persona.

### Attribution and exclusions

When using writing samples for calibration, keep these boundaries:

- Exclude editorial notes, labeled placeholders, missing code, image markers, isolated image captions, export escaping, and formatting damage from voice evidence.
- Exclude attributed specification and documentation quotations. Their wording is not evidence of the author's voice.
- In interviews, use the author's answers and introductory/closing prose, not the interviewer's questions or biographical fact lists, to infer style.
- Treat team-signed content as weaker evidence of individual authorship.
- Treat shared announcement templates as related evidence, not independent demonstrations of a universal personal habit.
- Historical technical assertions, emphatic statistics, biographical details, contact information, and program promises are not reusable facts merely because they occur in a sample.

Short examples illustrate voice moves. They are not required catchphrases or facts to reuse in a new draft.

## Supported voice traits

### 1. Cadence: detailed explanation followed by short checkpoints

**Confidence: high, especially in technical writing.**

Explanatory passages often carry several clauses, conditions, or parenthetical qualifications. Short sentences then reset the pace, acknowledge an outcome, or move the reader forward.

Examples: `Let's go!`, `Yes! It works!`, and `Well, it looks a bit broken.`

**Apply:** Allow a mechanism or tradeoff enough room to become clear, then use a brief checkpoint when something actually changes. Do not turn every sentence into a clipped statement, and do not manufacture alternating sentence lengths. Split a long sentence when it obscures the reasoning, not merely because it is long.

### 2. Vocabulary: precise technical nouns, conversational explanation

**Confidence: high.**

APIs, events, methods, tools, and configuration properties are named precisely. Around those identifiers, the connective language is conversational rather than academic. Definitions often translate a concept into what the reader can do or observe.

Examples: `In simple words` and `No secret and no magic`. An observable result can introduce the explanation before the underlying mechanism is unpacked.

**Apply:** Keep necessary domain terms and explain their role plainly. Do not replace a precise identifier with a vague business abstraction. Conversely, do not make ordinary connective language more formal simply to make the writing sound professional. Contractions are natural in the conversational registers.

### 3. Author presence: "I" for choices, "we" for exploration, "you" for the reader

**Confidence: high.**

First-person singular makes an experiment, preference, or limitation attributable. First-person plural brings the reader into the walkthrough. Direct address connects the explanation to the reader's next action.

Examples: `I decided to use Network-First strategy`, `Let's build a simple web application`, and `Just ask!`

**Apply:** Preserve the difference between a personal decision, a shared exercise, and an instruction. Do not make every sentence impersonal. Do not use "we" to invent an organizational position or shared experience; keep team and individual attribution separate.

### 4. Certainty: confident instruction with visible limits

**Confidence: high in technical and practical advice.**

The author can be decisive about a demonstrated step while marking an experimental feature, interpretation, or context-dependent recommendation. Enthusiasm and uncertainty coexist.

Examples: `It might be a better technical decision for real-life projects.` and `The set really depends on each specific case.`

**Apply:** Preserve distinctions between what was observed, what is suspected, what is preferred, and what is generally required. Keep limitations near the recommendation they qualify. Do not polish "might" into "will" or turn an experiment into a production guarantee.

This is not a uniformly cautious voice: opinion writing can make forceful recommendations, while announcements and endorsements allow stronger praise. Keep those register differences rather than averaging everything into either hedging or certainty.

### 5. Enthusiasm: discovery and useful results deserve a reaction

**Confidence: high; intensity is register-dependent.**

Technical passages openly enjoy a working demo, a new capability, or an elegant result. Short exclamations are genuinely part of the samples, not necessarily padding.

Examples: `Yes! It works!`, `And it works!`, and `I find it acceptable.` The last example also shows that not every outcome receives maximum praise.

**Apply:** Retain earned excitement attached to an actual result. Avoid replacing all warmth with neutral report language. Do not add excitement to every paragraph, invent an impressive outcome, or inflate a modest result into a breakthrough.

### 6. Humor: practical, playful, and sometimes self-directed

**Confidence: moderate across technical, personal, and opinion pieces.**

Humor includes small technical wordplays, self-deprecation, smileys, and occasional extended metaphors. It is not consistently dry or restrained.

Examples: `Let's cook a toast now.`, the self-directed `"genius"`, and `It works fine to train the memory though :)`. An extended metaphor can make a technical mechanism memorable when it remains useful.

**Apply:** Preserve a relevant aside when it carries warmth or helps explain the idea. Use an extended metaphor only where it remains useful. Do not add jokes, anecdotes, or smileys simply to signal personality. Stronger sarcasm is not the default tone for every audience.

### 7. Rhetorical habits: anticipate the reader's question, then answer it

**Confidence: high.**

Questions often introduce a real obstacle, distinction, or next step. The answer follows quickly, sometimes as a very short sentence, before the explanation continues.

Examples: `What's wrong? Nothing.` and `Where is the compromise then?` Plausible reader objections can introduce a useful distinction; likely questions can organize practical advice.

A useful variant acknowledges the valid part before drawing the distinction: `You are right, but`. Likewise, `Because it still works fine` preserves the value of an existing approach before introducing another option.

**Apply:** Keep questions that uncover a genuine uncertainty. Answer them, acknowledging a valid counterpoint when it helps explain the distinction. Do not manufacture agreement or soften every firm judgment to fit this sequence. Avoid scattering empty questions through otherwise complete prose or making every heading a question. Contrasts should explain an actual difference, not manufacture drama.

### 8. Punctuation and asides: conversational guidance inside the explanation

**Confidence: high for the devices; moderate for exact typography.**

Parentheses provide reminders, caveats, and side comments. Colons introduce definitions or practical lists. Dashes create asides or an explanatory turn. Question marks, exclamation marks, contractions, and occasional smileys contribute to the spoken quality.

Example: `(let's remember this name for later)`. A reader question and a parenthetical technical distinction can work together without making the explanation impersonal.

Occasional ellipses introduce a pause or reveal: `And... we are lucky`. Selective bold and italics highlight requirements or qualifications; capitals can emphasize a critical condition. These are optional devices serving a particular passage, not typography quotas.

**Apply:** Preserve an aside if removing it loses context, stance, or useful pacing. Keep punctuation readable and internally consistent, but do not ban dashes or parentheses as a house-style preference. Different dash characters, escaped punctuation, and unusual spacing in these exports do not establish an exact typography rule.

### 9. Reader benefit: connect mechanics to the experience they create

**Confidence: high across technical, UX, and community advice.**

The explanation often connects a technical or organizational choice to the burden or benefit for the person using it: interruptions, waiting, confusing flows, unnecessary steps, or intrusive communication.

Examples: `Put the user at the forefront.` and `try to minimize the number of emails`. Explain a choice through the benefit and burden it creates, not only its technical novelty.

**Apply:** Explain why a choice matters in the reader's or user's actual situation. Preserve the tradeoff rather than replacing it with a vague promise of a better experience. This is an observed argumentative focus, not a claim about the author's personality.

### 10. Structure: show the attempt, the result, and the catch

**Confidence: high in instructional writing.**

The samples frequently move from an achievable goal through implementation to observation, then address the imperfection or constraint the observation reveals. The route is not always a polished success story.

Examples: `Some observations:`, `Some important things to notice:`, and `Well, it looks a bit broken.`

Another recurring teaching move is to bound the scope and start with the simplest useful case: `We start from the very minimal solution`. Complexity is added deliberately as the example develops, often to address a limitation or explore another capability; a learning experiment need not be the simplest production choice.

**Apply:** Keep useful checkpoints, warnings, and explanations of why a first result is incomplete. Lists and headings usually organize steps, requirements, options, or caveats; they are not inherently unwanted scaffolding. Never reconstruct the removed code or claim an experiment was performed merely to reproduce this narrative shape.

Numbering does not always imply a sequence: it can identify setup steps or enumerate separate caveats. Preserve the relationship between items rather than imposing a rule that all numbered lists must be sequential.

### 11. Openings and endings: purpose and participation, not one fixed formula

**Confidence: high for variation; moderate for any single pattern.**

Some openings state the task immediately; others explain an experiment's occasion, establish a personal basis for advice, or build context before the recommendation. A universal answer-first rule would erase real differences.

Endings commonly point to a next experiment, invite questions, or welcome participation. Examples: `And I'll be happy to see your feedback in the comments.` and `I'll be happy to hear from you.`

**Apply:** Make the opening fit the reader's task. Retain a purposeful invitation where it belongs, but do not append a social-media call to action to every document. Tutorial summaries can be useful when they consolidate what was learned; delete only repetition that adds no reader benefit.

### 12. Credit: acknowledge the people behind the work

**Confidence: high in public technical writing.**

Appreciation is often specific to a contribution rather than generic praise. A phrase such as `express my gratitude and appreciation` is more useful when it identifies the contribution: implementation work, answers to questions, code review, or creating a tool or format.

**Apply:** Preserve relevant credit and the reason for it. When drafting, use only contributions and relationships supported by the task's facts; do not reuse historical acknowledgments as current endorsements. Do not add a thank-you, named contributor, or flattering introduction merely to imitate warmth.

## Register-specific differences

These are observed patterns, not compulsory templates. Choose the closest register before applying the general fingerprint.

### Technical walkthrough or implementation article

**Confidence: high.**

The reader has a concrete thing to build or understand. Use precise identifiers, a shared "we," occasional personal choices, and a visible sequence from setup to result. Requirements lists, warnings, demo checkpoints, and practical explanations belong naturally here.

The characteristic movement is goal -> working example -> explanation -> observed result -> caveat or improvement. Short celebrations can punctuate longer mechanism-heavy passages. Background is useful when it explains a choice; it need not precede the task every time.

**Protect:** technical distinctions, assumptions, demo-versus-production limits, and the sense of progressing alongside the reader.

**Avoid:** reducing the article to unexplained commands or turning it into a feature advertisement. Prose style alone does not establish coding-style preferences.

### Exploratory explainer or talk-like technical article

**Confidence: high within this register.**

Begin with a capability, question, or discovery, then explain the mechanism and test its limits. There is more room for analogy, a motivating story, and anticipated reader objections than in a narrow how-to.

The tone is curious and excited without pretending the technology is complete. Implementation problems, availability, and resource tradeoffs belong inside the explanation.

**Protect:** the distinction between a conceptual possibility, specification, observed implementation, and available feature.

**Avoid:** reusing historical browser-support claims as current facts or adding a dramatic discovery story that the author has not supplied.

### Community-organizing advice

**Confidence: moderate.**

Practical value and a personal basis for the advice arrive early. Question-led sections, concrete examples, useful lists, and small pro tips make the piece easy to act on. The voice addresses a peer organizer rather than an audience being sold a program.

Firm advice sits alongside case-by-case qualifications and respect for the other party. `Play fair in any case.` captures that balance.

**Protect:** reciprocity, realistic expectations, reader autonomy, and attention to how obtrusive a communication channel is.

**Avoid:** generic networking language or assuming advice about writing short emails proves what the author's own emails sound like.

### UX opinion or experience-led critique

**Confidence: provisional for this register; limited evidence.**

This register allows forceful, sometimes sarcastic argument grounded in a service goal, practical principles, concrete friction, and a positive counterexample. Best-practice and warning labels can make the recommendations explicit.

Make the basis of the argument explicit, for example: `Everything listed below is my personal opinion based on my personal experiences.`

**Protect:** the concrete friction, reader perspective, and distinction between experience and a general claim. Let strong judgments remain strong when they are part of the intended argument.

**Avoid:** spreading this level of indignation into neutral technical guidance or turning criticism of a specific flow into a personal attack. A constructive counterexample should offer a better experience, not merely a polite sign-off; `To finish the article on the positive note` can introduce that turn. Forceful recommendations are not permission to invent evidence or present an opinion as measured fact.

### Personal interview or career reflection

**Confidence: provisional for the register; limited evidence.**

The answers are first-person, conversational, and example-rich. They connect events and choices through a developing narrative rather than compressing everything into achievements. Tangents and self-deprecation can carry genuine voice.

Personal enthusiasm and concrete projects can explain motivation. A closing invitation, when useful, should be friendly and specific.

**Protect:** the author's own account, chronology, uncertainty, and the difference between a recollection and a current fact.

**Avoid:** turning every answer into a polished promotional biography, copying interviewer-written questions as evidence of the author's habits, or importing personal history into unrelated new writing.

### Book foreword or formal endorsement

**Confidence: provisional; limited evidence.**

The form is longer, connected paragraphs without the tutorial's frequent headings or checkpoints. It moves from first-hand technical context to the basis for trusting the book's author, then to the reader's benefit and a direct recommendation.

`Fast forward to our days.` is a conversational bridge that can fit an otherwise more formal piece. The tone remains warm and personal rather than becoming bureaucratic. Use restraint with humor and smileys rather than automatically transferring casual tutorial habits.

**Protect:** the actual relationship and experience supporting the endorsement, the subject's contribution, and the reason the book matters to its reader.

**Avoid:** treating this as evidence of an established executive-memo voice, or manufacturing praise, scale claims, relationships, and credentials to match the form.

### Program announcement or community campaign

**Confidence: provisional for individual voice; clearer support for the program register.**

A program announcement can move quickly from news to qualification criteria and an invitation to participate. Emoji, hashtags, collective language, and celebratory phrasing may fit this register without belonging in every personal message.

Examples: `To qualify, you must:` and `To qualify, you must verify at least one of the impacts in any of the bullets below.` Action-oriented guidance can instead offer a numbered set of choices, introduced with `What's next? You decide!`

**Protect:** the distinction between mandatory, optional, and alternative qualification conditions, and whether the speaker is the individual or the program team.

**Avoid:** treating shared campaign boilerplate as the author's signature wording or carrying emoji, hashtags, promotional promises, and organizational "we" into every register.

### Registers not established by these samples

There are no direct samples of internal email, Teams/chat, executive decision memos, sales proposals, incident reports, or apologies. Do not label recommendations for these forms as observed personal preferences.

No personal email greeting, email sign-off, or chat capitalization convention is established. Do not adopt a team signature as the author's personal signature, or turn the occasional `:)` into a default for private correspondence.

For such tasks, start with the actual reader, purpose, and delivery constraints. Practical detail, visible reasoning, warmth, and careful qualifications are plausible transferable elements. Reducing questions, shortening context, or lowering exuberance may be sensible editorial adaptations, but they remain adaptations until supported by matching samples.

## What to preserve, and what not to imitate

### Preserve when it serves the reader

- The visible practitioner: personal choices and observations attributed to "I," shared exploration attributed to "we."
- Mixed cadence, useful fragments, contractions, and brief checkpoints.
- Genuine questions that expose a distinction or move the explanation forward.
- Parenthetical qualifications, playful asides, and earned excitement.
- The link between implementation details and the experience they create.
- Specific appreciation and attribution when they are relevant to the piece.
- Honest limitations and the distinction between a demo, a preference, and a general recommendation.
- The target register's own pace and structure, including purposeful repetition for navigation or emphasis.

### Improve without flattening the voice

No paired rough and polished drafts were supplied. The cleanup suggestions here are editorial recommendations, not an observed account of how the author revises. Differences between genres do not establish a revision history.

Correct clear typos, broken grammar, duplicated words, missing articles, and awkward prepositions when doing so improves comprehension. These are not features to reproduce in order to sound authentic. Do not infer a linguistic or cultural identity from them.

Shorten an overloaded sentence when the claim or condition is hard to follow. Remove an aside when it adds no meaning, context, warmth, or pacing. A familiar transition or emphatic word is neither automatically protected nor automatically forbidden: judge its job in that passage.

Do not copy export backslashes, placeholders, unusual whitespace, or accidental spelling. Do not treat a quotation from another author, a UI label, a code identifier, or an interview prompt as freely editable authorial prose.

### Guard against a caricature

The fingerprint is not "add Let's, Awesome, a question, and a smiley." Those surface devices work because the underlying piece has an actual goal, an explained choice, a result, and something useful for the reader.

Descriptions of cadence and openings are not fixed recipes: neither alternating sentence lengths nor always putting experience before abstraction is required. A phrase repertoire is optional, not a checklist. Frequency or absence in this corpus does not establish a personal ban on semicolons, emoji, humor, or strong language; explicit user preferences govern.

Do not invent experiments, personal anecdotes, recommendations, experiences, jokes, or confidence to recreate the voice. Do not make the prose more provocative than its source. Preserve protected quotations, factual relationships, attribution, and uncertainty before optimizing style.

When a passage is already effective, leave it alone.

## Reusing and maintaining this guide

`professional-writing-editor` bundles this guide at `references\voice-profile.md`. Its `SKILL.md` requires on-demand loading for the user's own writing and authorized adaptations to the user's voice, not for neutral analysis, code or instruction maintenance, or work preserving another author's voice. The editor must load the full current file before proposing changes, identify the intended register, and repeat the voice comparison after register-specific edits.

Keep this guide with the skill when reusing it in another project; no project-level profile binding is needed. The profile governs style, not facts, editing scope, protected content, or permission to overwrite originals. If the required guide is unavailable, stop editing unless the user explicitly waives or replaces it.

Use this priority:

1. The current user's task, protected content, and delivery constraints.
2. Recent samples by the same author in the same register, including a clearly established voice in the target draft.
3. Same-author samples in adjacent registers and the supported traits in this guide.
4. More distant samples and generic style guidance.

If equally relevant samples disagree, preserve the target's established voice or resolve which example should govern. Do not blend conflicting voices into a generic compromise.

Before delivering an edit, check whether it has lost the author's presence, flattened the cadence, replaced ordinary words with formality, removed useful uncertainty, added unsupported enthusiasm, or imposed the wrong register. Use the full guidance here and any relevant samples provided for the current task, not only the compact fingerprint.

When revising this profile with new evidence, record the relevant register and observed pattern, distinguish a new preference from a one-off choice, and revisit confidence rather than silently universalizing it. Keep the guide understandable without external examples or document identifiers.
